# Negotiation

Use this skill when two or more parties want different outcomes from one situation.

This skill does not remove conflict. It does not replace problem analysis.

Negotiation includes emotion, instinct, incomplete information, and reason. Do not assume that reason controls each decision.

This framework uses methods associated with Chris Voss, a former Federal Bureau of Investigation hostage negotiator.

## Technical terms

These terms are approved for this skill:

- `active listening`: Careful listening that finds facts, needs, limits, and emotions.
- `mirroring`: Repetition of important words from the other party.
- `tactical empathy`: Recognition of the other party's view and emotions without necessary agreement.
- `emotion label`: A short statement that identifies a possible emotion or condition.
- `no-oriented question`: A question that lets the other party safely answer no.
- `calibrated question`: An open question that usually starts with what or how.
- `cognitive bias`: A predictable mental process that can affect a decision.
- `black swan`: Unexpected information that can materially change the negotiation.
- `positive leverage`: The ability to give something that the other party wants.
- `negative leverage`: The ability to prevent a loss that the other party wants to avoid.
- `normative leverage`: The use of the other party's stated rules or standards.
- `loss aversion`: The tendency to give a possible loss more importance than an equal possible gain.
- `reservation point`: The least favorable result that a party will accept.

## Principles

- Treat negotiation as a normal part of work and personal life.
- Listen before you make proposals.
- Find needs, limits, authority, timing, and emotions.
- Build trust with accurate understanding.
- Give each party genuine control over its decisions.
- Treat no as information, not as failure.
- Do not accept a midpoint only because it is a midpoint.
- Use deadlines only when they are real.
- Stay alert for unexpected information.
- Change the plan when new information changes the situation.
- Do not use deception, coercion, harassment, or unlawful threats.

## Preparation

Ask only for missing information that can materially change the plan.

Record these items:

1. State the desired result.
2. State the reasons for that result.
3. Set the reservation point.
4. State the best alternative without an agreement.
5. List known facts and important assumptions.
6. List each party's needs, losses, limits, and decision authority.
7. Identify real deadlines and their sources.
8. List information that can change the result.
9. Select the best communication channel.

Prefer an in-person meeting for sensitive or complex negotiations. Use a video or voice call when travel is not practical.

Written messages hide voice and body signals. Use them for records, simple proposals, and confirmed decisions.

## Roadmap

Prepare the negotiation in this order:

1. Write a neutral opening.
2. Prepare two or three emotion labels.
3. Prepare two or three mirrors from likely statements.
4. Prepare no-oriented questions.
5. Prepare calibrated questions.
6. Define the first proposal and its reasons.
7. Define possible exchanges and their conditions.
8. Define the reservation point and exit message.
9. List possible black swans and methods to find them.

Do not write a fixed script. Prepare flexible language and decision points.

## Conversation

### Start

Use a positive and calm voice as the normal voice. Speak with a smile when this behavior is suitable.

Use a slow voice with downward inflection for important limits. Do not use this voice to intimidate the other party.

State the purpose and invite correction.

Example: `I want to understand your priorities and find an agreement that works for both parties.`

### Listen and mirror

Listen for facts, needs, emotions, contradictions, and missing information.

Repeat one to three important words with a questioning tone. Then stop speaking.

Example:

- Other party: `The schedule creates too much risk.`
- Response: `Too much risk?`

Use mirroring to request more information. Do not use it after every statement.

### Apply tactical empathy

Observe words, voice changes, pauses, facial expressions, and body movement.

Apply an emotion label with `It sounds like`, `It seems like`, or `It looks like`.

Example: `It sounds like the approval process gives you limited flexibility.`

Stop speaking after the statement. Let the other party confirm, correct, or expand it.

Do not claim that you know the other party's internal state. Present each emotion label as a possible interpretation.

### Use no safely

Use no-oriented questions when the other party needs safety or decision control.

Examples:

- `Would it be unreasonable to review the schedule today?`
- `Are you opposed to a short trial?`
- `Is it a bad idea to define the approval date now?`

Accept no without punishment. Ask what the no protects or prevents.

Do not force a false choice. Genuine control is necessary for trust.

### Ask calibrated questions

Use what and how questions to find constraints and encourage joint analysis.

Examples:

- `What is the largest risk for you?`
- `How does your approval process work?`
- `What must occur before you can agree?`
- `How can I meet that condition within this schedule?`
- `What information am I missing?`

Avoid why when it can sound accusatory. Use `What causes that requirement?` instead.

## Proposals and exchanges

Do not make a concession without a reason or a condition.

Connect each exchange to a need.

Example: `I can reduce the first phase if we confirm the second phase by Friday.`

Do not split the difference automatically. Test whether the midpoint satisfies either party's important needs.

Use time carefully. A real deadline can support a decision, but a false deadline damages trust.

Use fair carefully. Ask for the other party's standard instead of using fair as an accusation.

Example: `What result would you consider fair, and what standard supports it?`

## Black swans and leverage

Search for information that can change authority, value, timing, risk, or available alternatives.

Ask questions during formal and informal discussions. Observe unexpected reactions and changes in voice or body movement.

When new information appears, ask why it matters now. Verify it before you change the plan.

Use positive leverage to exchange value. State what the other party receives and what you require.

Use normative leverage to compare actions with stated rules. Ask about the difference without accusation.

Example: `Your policy requires a five-day review. How does the current delay agree with that policy?`

Use negative leverage only to state real consequences. Never invent harm, expose private information, or threaten reputation.

Example: `If approval does not occur by Friday, I must release the reserved project time.`

## Review

After each discussion, record these items:

- New facts and corrected assumptions.
- Confirmed needs, limits, authority, and deadlines.
- Voice or body signals that need more examination.
- Proposals, conditions, and commitments.
- Possible black swans.
- The next action, owner, and date.

Confirm important agreements in writing. Separate confirmed terms from unresolved terms.

## Output

When the user requests negotiation help, provide only the necessary sections:

1. `Objective and limits`
2. `Other party assessment`
3. `Unknown information`
4. `Negotiation roadmap`
5. `Suggested language`
6. `Risks and exit point`

Keep uncertain statements explicit. Distinguish facts, assumptions, and interpretations.
